Implementation of MPPT for PV Arrays under Partial Shading Conditions

A working notebook · built from first principles · all curves computed from the single-diode model

This notebook starts at the physics of one silicon cell and ends at the algorithm you will implement. Nothing is assumed except school-level circuit theory. Every I-V and P-V curve in these pages is not a sketch: each one is solved numerically from the five-parameter single-diode equation for a real 265 W module, so the numbers you read off the axes are the numbers the model gives.

How the book is arranged

The order follows the physical build-up of the problem. Chapters 1 to 3 describe one cell and what moves its curve. Chapter 4 shows why a tracker is needed at all. Chapters 5 and 6 cover the tracking methods for a single panel. Chapter 7 assembles panels into an array and shows what must change. 1  The photovoltaic cell

1.1 What light does inside silicon

A solar cell is a large-area diode made from doped silicon. Its top layer is n-type: it has extra free electrons. Its lower, thicker layer is p-type: it has extra holes. Where the two layers meet, electrons diffuse into the p side and holes diffuse into the n side. They leave behind fixed charged atoms. This creates a thin region with no free carriers and a strong built-in electric field. This region is the depletion region, and its field is the working part of the cell.

Three things must happen for the cell to deliver current.

  1. Absorption. A photon enters the silicon and gives its energy to an electron. The photon must carry at least the band-gap energy, which is 1.12 eV for silicon. This corresponds to a wavelength of about 1100 nm. Photons with less energy pass through and do nothing. Photons with much more energy create one electron-hole pair and waste the surplus as heat.
  2. Separation. The freed electron and the hole it left behind must be pulled apart before they recombine. The built-in field of the depletion region does this. It pushes electrons toward the n side and holes toward the p side.
  3. Collection. The separated carriers must reach the metal contacts. The front contact is a grid of thin fingers, so it collects current without blocking much light. The rear contact usually covers the whole back surface.

1.2 Why a cell behaves as a current source with a diode across it

Two currents flow inside an illuminated cell at the same time, and they flow in opposite directions.

The first is the photocurrent Iph. It is set by how many usable photons arrive each second. Double the light, and you double the number of electron-hole pairs, so you double the photocurrent. The photocurrent is therefore almost exactly proportional to irradiance. It hardly depends on the cell's terminal voltage at all, because the built-in field sweeps the carriers out regardless.

The second is the ordinary diode current Id. The cell is still a p-n junction. When the terminals sit at a positive voltage, that voltage forward-biases the junction and pushes a recombination current back across it. This current grows exponentially with voltage.

The terminal current is the difference:

(1.1) I = Iph − Id(V)

That single line explains the whole shape of the I-V curve, and it is worth pausing on. At low terminal voltage the diode is barely conducting, so Id is negligible and I ≈ Iph. The cell looks like a current source. As the terminal voltage rises, Id grows exponentially and eats into the output. At one particular voltage the diode swallows the entire photocurrent, the terminal current reaches zero, and no more voltage is available. That voltage is the open-circuit voltage.

The one idea to carry forward

A solar cell is a light-controlled current source with a diode permanently connected across it. Light sets the height of the current source. The diode sets the voltage at which that current disappears. Everything else in this book follows from those two sentences.

2  The equivalent circuit and the I-V curve

2.1 The ideal cell

The simplest useful circuit has two elements: the photocurrent source and the junction diode.

Applying the Shockley diode equation to (1.1) gives the ideal characteristic:

(2.1) I = Iph − I0 · [ exp( V / (n·Vt) ) − 1 ]
Vt = k·T / q ≈ 25.7 mV at 25 °C

Here I0 is the reverse saturation current, which measures how leaky the junction is; n is the ideality factor, normally between 1 and 2; k is the Boltzmann constant; T is the absolute cell temperature; and q is the electron charge. Vt is the thermal voltage. For a module of Ns cells in series, replace n·Vt with n·Ns·Vt.

2.2 The five-parameter model

Two parasitic elements must be added before the model matches a real device.

Series resistance Rs collects every ohmic loss along the current path: the bulk silicon, the emitter sheet, the contact between metal and silicon, the fingers, the busbars and the ribbons. It is small, usually a few hundred milliohms for a module, but the current through it is large.

Shunt resistance Rsh represents every leakage path that lets current bypass the junction: edge leakage around the cell perimeter, cracks, and defects that short the junction locally. It should be large. A poor cell has a low shunt resistance.

Writing Kirchhoff's current law at the internal node gives the equation that the rest of the book uses:

(2.2) I = Iph − I0 · [ exp( (V + I·Rs) / a ) − 1 ] − (V + I·Rs) / Rsh
a = n · Ns · k · T / q   (the modified ideality factor)

Equation 2.2 is implicit: I appears on both sides and inside an exponential. It has no solution in elementary functions. In practice you solve it point by point with a root-finder, or in closed form with the Lambert W function. Every curve in this notebook was solved with a bracketing root-finder to a tolerance of 10−12 A.

2.3 The four landmarks of the curve

Four points describe almost everything you need.

Short-circuit current, Isc
The current when the terminals are shorted, so V = 0. The diode is unbiased and takes almost nothing, so Isc ≈ Iph. This is the largest current the cell can deliver. It is proportional to irradiance.
Open-circuit voltage, Voc
The voltage when no current is drawn, so I = 0. The diode is now consuming the whole photocurrent. Setting I = 0 in 2.1 and solving gives Voc = a·ln(Iph/I0 + 1). This is the largest voltage the cell can produce.
Maximum power point (MPP), at Vmp and Imp
The single point on the curve where the product V·I is largest. At both ends of the curve the power is zero, because one factor is zero. Somewhere between them there is a maximum. Finding and holding this point is the entire subject of this project.
Fill factor, FF
How square the curve is: FF = (Vmp·Imp) / (Voc·Isc). A perfect rectangle would give 1. Good silicon modules reach 0.75 to 0.82. Laboratory cells reach about 0.85. A falling fill factor is the standard symptom of a resistance problem.

Why the MPP must exist

Power is zero at V = 0 because there is no voltage. Power is zero at V = Voc because there is no current. Power is positive in between, and the curve is smooth. A continuous function that is zero at both ends and positive in the middle must have a maximum inside. Under uniform light there is exactly one such maximum. Under partial shade there can be several, and that is the whole difficulty of this project.

2.4 The two-diode model

The single-diode model is slightly wrong at low light. The reason is that recombination in the depletion region follows a different law from recombination in the bulk. The two-diode model gives each mechanism its own diode: one with n1 ≈ 1 for the bulk, and one with n2 ≈ 2 for the depletion region.

2.5 What the parasitic resistances do to the curve

The two resistances damage two different parts of the curve, and this is how you tell them apart on a flash-test report.

Series resistance pulls the curve down near the open-circuit end. It costs you a voltage of I·Rs at every operating point, and the current is highest at the knee, so the knee suffers most. Voc is untouched, because at open circuit no current flows through Rs. Isc is barely touched. The fill factor falls.

Shunt resistance tilts the flat part of the curve near short circuit. A low Rsh steals current in proportion to voltage, so the curve slopes downward instead of running flat. Isc is barely touched, but Voc falls and the fill factor falls.

Diagnostic rule

Look at the slope of the measured curve at the two ends. The slope near Voc is roughly −1/Rs. The slope near Isc is roughly −1/Rsh. Those two slopes are the fastest field estimate of both parasitic resistances, and they need no curve fitting.

3  How light and heat move the curve

Two variables in the field change the curve minute by minute: the irradiance falling on the plane of the module, and the cell temperature. They act on different parts of the curve. This is the section that answers the question what sags, and what stays put.

3.1 Irradiance: the current scales, the knee barely moves

Irradiance G sets the photocurrent directly. The relation is close to linear:

(3.1) Iph(G, T) = (G / 1000) · [ Iph,STC + αI · (T − 25 °C) ]

Because Isc ≈ Iph, the short-circuit current follows the light almost exactly. Cut the irradiance to one fifth, and the short-circuit current falls to one fifth. The model confirms this to within rounding.

Read the two percentage columns side by side. A ten-to-one drop in light gives a ten-to-one drop in current, but only an 11 % drop in voltage. Current is the quantity that sags. Voltage is the quantity that holds.

3.2 Why Voc moves so little

The reason is worth deriving, because the whole fractional-voltage family of trackers rests on it. Set I = 0 in the ideal equation and solve for V:

(3.2) Voc = a · ln( Iph / I0 + 1 ) ≈ a · ln( Iph / I0 )

Now change the irradiance from G1 to G2. Only Iph changes, and it changes in proportion, so:

(3.3) Voc(G2) − Voc(G1) = a · ln( G2 / G1 )

The voltage difference is logarithmic in the irradiance ratio, while the current difference is linear. For the reference module, a = 1.77 V. Halving the light therefore costs 1.77 × ln 2 = 1.23 V, which is 3.3 % of Voc. Halving the light costs 50 % of the current. That is the entire asymmetry in one line.

Answer to the question “what is constant in dim and strong light”

Nothing is exactly constant, but three quantities are nearly so:

  • the knee voltage, and therefore Voc and Vmp, which move only logarithmically;
  • the ratio Vmp/Voc, which stays between 0.826 and 0.845 over a ten-to-one light range on the reference module;
  • the ratio Imp/Isc, which stays between 0.928 and 0.930 over the same range.

The strongly varying quantities are Isc, Imp and Pmax, all of which are proportional to irradiance.

3.3 Temperature: the voltage collapses, the current creeps up

Temperature works the other way round. The band gap narrows slightly as silicon heats, so marginally more photons are absorbed and Isc rises a little. At the same time the reverse saturation current I0 rises steeply, because it depends on exp(−Eg/kT). A larger I0 means the diode swallows the photocurrent at a lower voltage, so Voc falls.

4  Why maximum power point tracking exists

4.1 The load picks the operating point, not the panel

A panel does not choose where on its curve it sits. The load does. Connect a resistor R across the terminals, and the operating point is the intersection of the panel curve with the straight line I = V/R. Connect a battery, and the panel is clamped to the battery voltage, so the operating point is wherever the curve crosses that vertical line.

The figure makes the loss concrete. The reference module can deliver 265 W at STC. Wire it directly to a nominal 24 V lead-acid battery, which sits near 24 V while charging, and the panel is forced to 24 V. It then delivers 9.08 A, which is 217.8 W. You have thrown away 47 W, or 17.8 %, with no fault anywhere in the system.

4.2 The real statement: impedance matching

At the maximum power point the panel behaves, instantaneously, like a source feeding an optimum resistance:

(4.1) Ropt = Vmp / Imp = 30.97 / 8.559 = 3.62 Ω  (at STC)

Maximum power point tracking is therefore an impedance-matching problem. The tracker's job is to make the source see 3.62 Ω no matter what is really connected. And Ropt itself moves: at 200 W/m² it becomes 29.27 / 1.712 = 17.1 Ω, roughly five times larger. So the matching must be continuous, not a one-time adjustment.

4.3 The switching converter as an adjustable impedance

You cannot change the battery, and you cannot change the panel. You put a DC-DC converter between them. Seen from the panel, a lossless converter feeding a fixed load looks like a resistor whose value you set with the duty ratio D.

For a lossless converter driving a load of resistance R, the input resistance seen by the panel is:

(4.2) buck:     Rin = R / D²
boost:    Rin = R · (1 − D)²
buck-boost:  Rin = R · [ (1 − D) / D ]²

The buck can only make the source see a resistance smaller than the load. The boost can only make it look larger. The buck-boost can do both, which is why it, or a SEPIC, or a Cuk, is chosen when the panel voltage may sit on either side of the bus voltage. For a battery load, the same algebra applies to the equivalent operating resistance Vbat/Ibat.

4.4 The tracking loop and its timing

Every tracker, however clever, is the same three steps repeated forever.

  1. Sense. Measure the array voltage and the array current. Multiply to get power. Two sensors is the usual minimum. Some methods need only one.
  2. Decide. Compare the new measurement with the previous one, or with a model, and choose a new operating point.
  3. Act. Move the duty ratio, or move the reference voltage that an inner loop then regulates.

The timing of step 3 is the part most often got wrong in a first design. After you change the duty ratio, the input capacitor, the inductor and the array capacitance need time to settle. If you sample before the transient has decayed, you measure the transient and not the new operating point, and the algorithm makes a decision on noise. The perturbation period must be several times the settling time of the power stage. For a small converter this is typically 1 ms to 50 ms. This single number, the settling time, is what makes the search question in Chapter 11 interesting: it, and not processor speed, is the real cost of one trial point.

Control variable: duty ratio or reference voltage?

Perturbing D directly is simplest and needs no inner loop. Perturbing a reference voltage Vref, with a PI loop that forces Vpv to follow it, is slower to design but far better behaved: the step size in volts then means the same thing at every operating point, and the loop rejects load disturbances. Most production trackers use the reference-voltage form.

4.5 How a tracker is scored

Two numbers, and they are not the same.

(4.3) ηMPPT = ∫ Pactual(t) dt ⁄ ∫ Pmax,available(t) dt

Static tracking efficiency is measured with the light held constant. It is limited by the steady-state oscillation around the peak. A well-tuned hill climber reaches 99 % or better.

Dynamic tracking efficiency is measured while the light changes on a defined ramp, as in the EN 50530 test profiles. It is limited by how fast the tracker can follow. This is the number that separates good trackers from poor ones, and it is where a naive perturb-and-observe loses badly.

For a shading study you need a third number: the global tracking success rate, which is the fraction of shading patterns in which the tracker settles on the true global peak rather than a local one. A tracker can score 99 % static efficiency and still be sitting on the wrong peak, losing a third of the available power.

5  Tracking methods I: the offline family

5.1 A map of the families

There are more than a hundred published tracking methods. Almost all of them belong to one of three families, and the family tells you the strengths and the weaknesses before you read a single equation.

The offline family shares one idea: the maximum power point is not measured, it is estimated from something cheaper. That estimate is then used as a set-point. The panel is never asked whether the estimate was right.

5.2 Fractional open-circuit voltage

This method rests on the observation from Section 3.2: the ratio Vmp/Voc is nearly constant.

(5.1) Vmp ≈ kv · Voc    with kv ≈ 0.71 to 0.86 for silicon

For the reference module the model gives kv between 0.798 and 0.861 across the full range of 100 to 1000 W/m² and 0 to 65 °C. Choosing a fixed kv = 0.82 costs at most 1.85 % of the available power anywhere on that grid, and less than 0.7 % over most of it. That is a remarkable result for a method that needs one sensor and two lines of arithmetic.

How it runs. Every few seconds the converter is stopped, or a pilot cell is read, so that Voc can be measured. The set-point kv·Voc is then held until the next sample.

Fractional Voc
AdvantagesOne voltage sensor. No multiplication, no division, no memory of past states. Cannot oscillate. Cannot become unstable. Reaches the neighbourhood of the peak in one step, which makes it a good starting stage for a two-stage tracker.
DisadvantagesNever exactly at the peak. The array must be disconnected to measure Voc, which loses all power for a few milliseconds each time. kv drifts with ageing, temperature and technology. Under partial shade the relation breaks down completely, because Voc no longer describes the shape of a multi-peak curve.
Where it fitsVery small, very cheap systems: solar lanterns, garden lights, water pumps. Also the coarse first stage of a hybrid tracker. Also as a safe fallback if sensors fail.

5.3 Fractional short-circuit current

The mirror image. The ratio Imp/Isc is even steadier than the voltage ratio: the model holds it between 0.928 and 0.930 across the whole irradiance range.

(5.2) Imp ≈ ki · Isc    with ki ≈ 0.78 to 0.93

Using a fixed ki = 0.929 on the reference module costs at most 0.26 % of the available power. It is the more accurate of the two fractional methods.

Fractional Isc
AdvantagesMore stable coefficient than the voltage method. Insensitive to temperature.
DisadvantagesMeasuring Isc means shorting the array. That needs an extra switch rated for the full array current, which costs more than it saves. A current sensor is more expensive than a voltage divider. Same blindness to partial shade.
Where it fitsSystems that already have a short-circuit-capable switch, or that carry a small pilot cell whose Isc can be measured without disturbing the array.

5.4 Look-up tables and curve fitting

If you measure irradiance and cell temperature, you can look up Vmp in a table filled during commissioning, or evaluate a fitted polynomial in G and T. The response is immediate, because there is no search at all.

Look-up and curve fitting
AdvantagesFastest possible response to a change: one table read. No oscillation. Predictable behaviour, which suits certification.
DisadvantagesNeeds a pyranometer and a temperature sensor, both of which cost more than the tracker. The table is valid for one array, in one place, in one state of cleanliness and age. It cannot represent shading patterns at all, because the shading pattern is not a scalar.
Where it fitsLarge plants that already carry weather instruments, used as a coarse pre-position for a fine tracker. Space systems, where the environment is known exactly and reliability outranks accuracy.

6  Tracking methods II: hill climbing and beyond

The online family does not guess. It moves the operating point, watches what happens to the power, and uses the result to decide the next move. It is exact at steady state, and it needs no knowledge of the panel. It is also, without help, strictly local: it climbs the hill it is standing on.

6.1 Perturb and observe

The oldest and still the most used method. The rule is one sentence: change the operating voltage by a small step; if the power went up, take another step the same way; if the power went down, step the other way.

The step size is the whole design. A large step reaches the peak quickly but then oscillates across it with a large amplitude, wasting power in steady state. A small step settles tightly but takes many cycles to cross the curve, and responds slowly to a passing cloud. The reference module gives a feel for the cost: sitting 1 V away from Vmp costs 1.0 % of peak power, 2 V costs 5.0 %, and 3 V costs 13.4 %. The curve is flat at the top and steep on the right shoulder, so an overshoot to the right is much more expensive than the same overshoot to the left.

The drift problem. Perturb and observe cannot tell the difference between “the power rose because I moved” and “the power rose because a cloud cleared”. On a fast irradiance ramp it therefore reads a rising power, concludes its last move was correct, and keeps moving in a direction that is in fact wrong. The operating point drifts away from the peak. The standard cures are:

  • Variable step size. Make the step proportional to |dP/dV|, so it is large far from the peak and small near it. This is the single most effective improvement, and it is cheap.
  • Three-point weight comparison. Sample at three points before deciding, which rejects a monotone irradiance ramp.
  • Add a current measurement test. A change in I at constant V indicates irradiance change, not the effect of the perturbation.
Perturb and observe
AdvantagesNo model of the panel. No calibration. Works with any array. Trivial to code and to test. Reaches the true peak, not an estimate. Static tracking efficiency above 99 % with a good step size.
DisadvantagesAlways oscillates around the peak. Drifts on fast irradiance ramps. Needs both sensors. Stops at the first peak it finds, which under partial shade is very often the wrong one.
Where it fitsThe default for anything without shading. The local-refinement stage of nearly every global method, including the one recommended in Chapter 11.

6.2 Incremental conductance

This method uses the same hill, but computes the slope analytically instead of comparing two power readings. Differentiate P = V·I with respect to V:

(6.1) dP/dV = I + V · dI/dV

At the maximum power point the slope is zero, which gives the test that names the method:

(6.2) at the MPP:   dI/dV = − I / V
left of it:    dI/dV > − I / V   (increase V)
right of it:   dI/dV < − I / V   (decrease V)

In words: the incremental conductance dI/dV is compared with the negative of the instantaneous conductance I/V. In an implementation the derivatives become differences, ΔI/ΔV.

Incremental conductance
AdvantagesHas a true stop condition, so it can stop oscillating once the peak is reached. Distinguishes a change caused by its own perturbation from a change caused by irradiance, because it looks at ΔV and ΔI separately. Better dynamic tracking efficiency than plain P&O on ramps.
DisadvantagesNeeds a division, so more computation and more care with fixed-point arithmetic. The exact equality is never met with real ADC noise, so a tolerance band must be added — and that band re-introduces a small oscillation. Still strictly local.
Where it fitsThe standard choice for grid-tied string inverters and for anything where irradiance changes quickly. Also the usual refinement stage in global methods.

6.3 Hill climbing on the duty ratio

Identical logic to perturb and observe, but the perturbed variable is the duty ratio itself rather than a voltage reference. It removes the inner voltage loop, which saves a controller and some tuning.

The price is that the relation between duty ratio and array voltage is non-linear and depends on the load. A fixed step in D gives a small voltage step in one part of the range and a large one in another. The tracker is therefore well damped at one operating point and twitchy at another.

6.4 Ripple correlation control

A switching converter already makes the array voltage and current ripple at the switching frequency. Ripple correlation control uses that ripple as the perturbation, so no artificial perturbation is needed at all.

The insight is a sign test. Below the peak, power and voltage ripple in phase, so their time derivatives have the same sign. Above the peak they are in antiphase. The control law is therefore:

(6.3) dD/dt = − k · ( dP/dt ) · ( dV/dt )    k > 0

The product is positive below the peak and negative above it, so the duty ratio is driven until the product averages to zero, which happens only at the peak.

Ripple correlation control
AdvantagesConverges in a few switching periods, so it is one to three orders of magnitude faster than perturb and observe. No added perturbation, so no added steady-state loss. Proven to converge.
DisadvantagesNeeds analogue or fast-sampled measurement of the ripple, which means wideband sensing and careful layout. Sensitive to phase shift in the sensing chain: a filter that delays the ripple can invert the sign of the product and make the loop climb the wrong way. Still local.
Where it fitsModule-level converters and any design where the tracker must follow fast-moving shade edges. Attractive for a hardware-focused final year project, because the interesting work is analogue.

6.5 Extremum seeking control

A small sinusoidal dither at frequency ω is added to the set-point. The measured power is multiplied by the same sinusoid and low-pass filtered. The result is proportional to the local gradient, with the correct sign. An integrator then drives the gradient to zero. It is the formal, provable version of hill climbing, and it separates the estimation frequency from the control bandwidth, which is what gives it its good noise behaviour.

6.6 Fuzzy logic control

Instead of a fixed step size, the step is decided by a rule base. Two inputs are usual: the error E = ΔP/ΔV and its change ΔE. Each is mapped to linguistic labels such as negative big, zero, positive small. A rule table then selects a change in duty ratio, and the result is defuzzified to a number.

In practice a fuzzy controller is a smooth, non-linear, gain-scheduled hill climber. It takes large steps far from the peak and small steps near it, which is exactly the variable step size that plain perturb and observe wants, but expressed in a form that is easier to tune by hand than a formula.

Fuzzy logic
AdvantagesNo panel model. Handles the non-linearity gracefully. Small steady-state oscillation and fast approach at the same time. Tolerant of sensor noise.
DisadvantagesThe rule base and membership functions are chosen by the designer, so performance depends on skill and on trial. More memory and computation than P&O. Still local unless combined with a global search.
Where it fitsSystems with fast-changing conditions where the tuning effort is justified. Common as the refinement half of a hybrid global tracker.

6.7 Neural networks

A network is trained offline to map inputs such as irradiance, temperature, Voc and Isc onto Vmp. At run time it is a fixed function evaluation, so the response is immediate.

Neural network
AdvantagesOnce trained, it jumps straight to the answer with no search. Can absorb effects that no simple formula captures, including the array's own shading geometry if it was in the training set.
DisadvantagesNeeds a large, representative training set from the actual array. Must be retrained as the array ages or is modified. Behaves unpredictably outside the training distribution, which is precisely where an unusual shading pattern lives.
Where it fitsLarge installations that can afford the data collection, or as a pre-positioning stage that a hill climber then refines.

6.8 Sliding mode and model predictive control

Sliding mode control defines a switching surface, usually S = dP/dV = 0, and applies a discontinuous control law that forces the state onto that surface and keeps it there. It is robust against parameter error and load change, and it has a formal stability proof. Its weakness is chattering at the switching surface, which must be softened in practice.

Model predictive control uses a model of the converter to predict the array power for each candidate switching state over a short horizon, then applies the state that maximises a cost function. It gives an excellent dynamic response with no perturbation at all, but it needs an accurate converter model and a processor fast enough to evaluate the predictions inside one switching period.

6.10 How to choose

The decision is usually made by three questions, in this order.

  1. Can the array be shaded in normal use? If yes, no method in this chapter is sufficient on its own. You need a global stage from Chapter 10 with one of these as the refinement stage.
  2. How fast does the irradiance change? A fixed installation in a clear climate can use plain perturb and observe. Moving shade edges, or a vehicle-mounted array, need incremental conductance, ripple correlation, or a variable step.
  3. What is the cost target? Below a few watts, the fractional methods win because they need one sensor. Above that, the sensors are already paid for, so the arithmetic is free.

7  From one panel to an array

Everything so far described a single, uniformly lit device. A real installation is dozens of modules wired together, and the wiring changes the problem in ways that are not obvious.

7.1 Series, parallel, and how the curves combine

Two rules govern every combination, and both follow from Kirchhoff's laws.

  • In series, the current is common and the voltages add. To build the combined curve, pick a current, read each device's voltage at that current, and add the voltages.
  • In parallel, the voltage is common and the currents add. Pick a voltage, read each device's current at that voltage, and add the currents.

A standard module is itself an application of the first rule: 60 or 72 cells in series, so that one module produces 30 to 45 V. Half-cut modules cut each cell in two and use 120 or 144 half cells in two parallel halves; this halves the current in each cell and therefore quarters the resistive loss, and it also gives useful shading behaviour, as Section 8.4 explains.

7.2 Mismatch: the reason arrays are worse than the sum of their parts

No two modules are identical. The differences are called mismatch, and they matter because of the series rule above.

The last row is different in kind, not only in size. The others shift a curve slightly. Shade removes a whole section of a string from service, and that is what creates the multiple peaks that this project must handle.

7.3 Where the tracker sits

One tracker cannot optimise two modules that disagree. It can only choose one operating current for the whole series path. The system architecture therefore decides how much mismatch loss you are prepared to accept.

The size of the prize is easy to state with the reference module. Take three modules in series at 1000, 600 and 300 W/m². Their individual peak powers add to 499 W. A single tracker on the series string can extract only 337 W, because it must choose one current for all three. Module-level electronics would recover the difference, less its own conversion loss. That gap, about one third, is what optimiser manufacturers sell.

The important consequence for this project

If the array has one tracker for many modules in series, then partial shading produces a multi-peak curve and you must solve a global search problem. If every module has its own tracker, each sees a single peak and ordinary perturb and observe is enough. The algorithm you need is determined by the wiring, not by the weather.

7.4 Adapting a single-panel algorithm to an array

The equations do not change. Five practical things do.

  1. Scale the step size. A step of 0.5 V is sensible on a 37 V module. On a 600 V string it is far too small: the tracker would need hundreds of cycles to cross the curve. Express the step as a fraction of array Voc, typically 0.2 % to 1 %, so the same code works at any array size.
  2. Lengthen the settling time. Array capacitance and cable inductance both grow with size. Sample later, not sooner.
  3. Do not trust a single power reading. With long cables, noise on the current sensor is worse. Average several samples before comparing.
  4. Bound the search. The useful range is roughly 0.5 to 0.95 of array Voc. Refusing to leave that window prevents the tracker from wandering to short circuit after a disturbance.
  5. Add a shading test. Any array with more than one module in series needs a periodic check that it is still on the global peak. Chapter 10 covers this.

7.5 Array wiring topologies

Modules can be interconnected in several patterns. The pattern changes how a shadow spreads through the array, and therefore how much power is lost.

The ranking under shade is usually TCT first, then bridge-linked and honeycomb, then plain series-parallel. TCT wins because the cross ties let current from an unshaded module in the same row bypass the shaded one, so the shadow degrades the row rather than killing the string. The cost is more wiring, more terminations and higher fault currents.

8  What partial shade does to the circuit

8.1 Where shade comes from

Partial shading means that the modules of one array receive different irradiance at the same moment. The common causes divide into two groups by time scale.

CausePatternDurationPredictable?
Passing cloud edgesoft, moving, largeseconds to minutesno
Chimney, pole, antenna, satellite dishhard, narrow, moving with the sun1 to 3 h dailyyes
Neighbouring building or treehard, large, seasonalhours, worse in winteryes
Row-to-row self shadinghard, horizontal band, from the bottommorning and eveningyes
Bird droppings, leaves, snow patcheshard, very small, fixeddays to weeksno
Uneven soilingsoft, largeweekspartly

A hard shadow is the dangerous kind. A cloud reduces irradiance to perhaps 20 % over a wide area, so the modules stay similar to one another. A pole casts a sharp shadow that puts one cell at 5 % while its neighbour stays at 100 %. That difference, inside one series group, is what does the damage.

8.2 The series bottleneck

Recall the series rule: the current is common. A cell that receives one fifth of the light can generate only one fifth of the photocurrent. If the string tries to carry more current than that, the shaded cell simply cannot supply it from light. The extra current must still pass through the cell, because there is nowhere else for it to go.

To pass a current larger than its photocurrent, the cell must go to a negative terminal voltage. It stops being a source and becomes a load. This is the single most important sentence in this chapter.

8.3 Reverse bias and the hot spot

Follow what happens as the string current is pushed above the shaded cell's photocurrent. The cell voltage falls through zero and goes negative. At first the reverse current is limited by the shunt resistance. Then, at a reverse voltage of typically 10 to 30 V for a silicon cell, avalanche breakdown begins and the cell conducts freely in reverse.

The cell is now dissipating |V| × I as heat. Two ceilings limit that number:

  • the reverse voltage at which the cell breaks down, which is a property of the cell;
  • the voltage the healthy cells in the same bypass group can produce, because they are the source driving the current.

For a 20-cell group at the reference operating point, the healthy cells can generate about 20 × 0.516 V × 8.56 A ≈ 88 W. Nearly all of that can end up in one shaded cell, in an area of about 150 cm², under a glass sheet. Cell temperatures above 150 °C are reported in hot-spot tests. The consequences are delamination, cracked glass, browning of the encapsulant, melted solder joints and, in the worst case, fire.

8.4 Bypass diodes

The cure is to give the current another path. A bypass diode is connected across a group of cells, with the opposite polarity to the cells. In normal operation the group produces a positive voltage, so the diode is reverse biased and does nothing. When the group is forced negative by about 0.5 V, the diode conducts and carries the string current around the group. The group's reverse voltage is then clamped to one diode drop instead of tens of volts.

Two design numbers matter.

Group size. Protection improves as the group gets smaller, because a smaller group can generate less power to dump into a shaded cell. The classic guidance is a maximum of about 15 cells per diode for full protection. Commercial practice uses 20 to 24 cells per diode, which is a compromise between safety and the cost of diodes and junction-box terminals. Half-cut modules improve on this without more diodes, because each half carries half the current.

Diode dissipation. A conducting bypass diode carries the entire string current. At 8.56 A, a Schottky diode at 0.45 V dissipates 3.9 W; a PN diode at 0.9 V dissipates 7.7 W. That heat is generated inside a sealed plastic junction box on the back of a hot module. Bypass diodes are one of the most common field failures, and they fail in two ways:

  • Short circuit, the usual thermal-runaway outcome. The substring is permanently removed, so the module loses a third of its output and nobody notices for months.
  • Open circuit, often after a lightning surge. The protection is gone, and the next hard shadow produces a hot spot.

8.5 What actually gets overloaded

It is worth being precise here, because “the shaded module overloads the others” is a common but loose statement. The healthy modules are not driven beyond their ratings by a shadow. Three real overloads do occur.

  1. Thermal overload of the shaded cell. Described above. This is the dangerous one, and bypass diodes exist to prevent it.
  2. Thermal overload of the bypass diode. Several watts, continuously, in a sealed box, whenever the shadow is present. Long daily shading from a fixed obstruction is much harder on a diode than an occasional cloud.
  3. Reverse current into a weak parallel string. If several strings are paralleled and one string's voltage collapses under shade, the healthy strings drive current backwards into it. The weak string then dissipates instead of generating.

There is also a fourth effect which is not an overload but is often mistaken for one. When the tracker sits on a low-voltage peak, the string current is close to Isc while the string voltage is low. Cables, connectors and fuses therefore carry near-maximum current while the array delivers little power. Nothing is damaged, but the resistive losses are as high as at full output, and the useful output is not.

8.6 Parallel strings and blocking diodes

A blocking diode in series with each string prevents reverse current from entering a weak or shaded string. It solves item 3 above. Its cost is a permanent forward drop in every string, at all times, which is a continuous loss of roughly 0.5 V out of several hundred. Modern practice usually prefers string fuses, or simply an inverter with one tracker per string, because a fuse costs nothing when nothing is wrong. Blocking diodes remain common in small battery systems, where they also stop the battery discharging into the array at night.

8.7 Damage, standards and ageing

Hot-spot endurance is a type-approval requirement. In IEC 61215 the hot-spot test (MQT 09) deliberately shades cells and runs the module at short circuit for several hours to show that it survives. Passing that test does not mean shading is harmless; it means a single event will not destroy the module.

Repeated shading shortens life in slower ways as well: thermal cycling of solder joints, local encapsulant browning, and accelerated degradation of the affected cells. A module that is shaded for two hours every day for twenty years experiences about 15 000 hot-spot cycles.

Design consequence

A global tracking algorithm is not only an energy-yield feature. By keeping the array off operating points where a shaded substring is deeply reverse biased, and by choosing a peak on the correct side of a bypass transition, the tracker also influences how much heat is put into the weakest cell. Tracking and reliability are not separate problems.

9  The multi-peak characteristic

9.1 How the staircase forms

Now put the two rules together. Take three modules in series at 1000, 600 and 300 W/m², each with a bypass diode. Sweep the string current from zero upward and see what each module contributes.

  • Below 2.76 A (the short-circuit current of the 300 W/m² module), all three modules generate. All three voltages add. The string behaves normally, but at the current of the weakest module.
  • Between 2.76 and 5.52 A, the weakest module cannot supply the current. Its bypass diode conducts. It now contributes about −0.7 V instead of +30 V. The string voltage drops by roughly 31 V in one step.
  • Above 5.52 A, the middle module drops out in the same way, and only the fully lit module remains.

Each bypass transition is a step down in voltage at almost constant current. The I-V curve becomes a staircase.

Multiply point by point by the voltage and the staircase becomes a range of hills.

9.2 Counting and placing the peaks

Two rules, both useful when you write the search algorithm.

(9.1) number of local peaks ≤ number of distinct irradiance levels in the string
number of local peaks ≤ number of bypass-diode groups in series

Both bounds hold at once, so the smaller applies. A string of four modules with one bypass diode each has at most four peaks, whatever the shadow does. This is the fact that makes the search tractable: the number of candidates is small, known in advance, and fixed by the hardware.

The second rule concerns position. Each peak occurs near the point where one more group has just been bypassed, so the peaks appear at roughly regular voltage intervals of about one module's Vmp. Published surveys place them at multiples of 70 % to 85 % of the module open-circuit voltage, with the two rightmost peaks between 75 % and 95 %. The model agrees: for a four-module string at 1000, 800, 450 and 250 W/m², the peaks fall at 77 %, 83 %, 88 % and 89 % of k × Voc,module.

Read the last row carefully, because it is the most instructive. One heavily shaded module out of four costs the string 26 % of its power, and the best operating point is now at 92 V instead of 124 V. A tracker that stayed near the old voltage would find the smaller peak and lose much more.

9.3 Why the classical trackers fail

Perturb and observe and incremental conductance both use only local information: the sign of dP/dV at the present operating point. On a multi-peak curve that sign tells you which way the nearest hilltop is. It says nothing about whether a taller hill exists elsewhere. Once the tracker reaches any hilltop, dP/dV = 0, and every rule it has says stop.

The cost is not small. For the three-module string above:

A quarter of the available power, lost by an algorithm that is working exactly as designed. And which peak the tracker lands on depends only on where it happened to be when the shadow arrived, so the loss is unpredictable from day to day.

9.4 How much energy is really at stake

Three different losses are often confused. Separating them tells you which one your project can actually address.

Field studies of shaded residential arrays generally report annual gains between 3 % and 15 % from global tracking, depending on how often the array is shaded.

10  Detecting shade and finding the global peak

A global tracker has two jobs that are usually confused with one another. The first is to notice that the curve has changed shape. The second is to search the new curve. They need different methods, and a design that does the second well but the first badly will still perform poorly, because it will either search too often or too late.

10.1 Detecting the change

A search is expensive: while you sweep, you are not at the peak, so you lose energy. A search is also unnecessary most of the time. The detector decides when it is worth paying.

TriggerRuleComment

Choosing the threshold

Too low, and the tracker rescans on every gust of cloud, spending more energy on searching than it recovers. Too high, and it sits on a local peak all afternoon. A practical rule is to set the power threshold just above the largest step the tracker's own perturbation can produce, then rely on the periodic timer to catch anything the threshold misses.

10.2 Scanning methods

The most direct global method is to look at the whole curve. Sweep the reference voltage from near Voc down to a low value, record the power at each step, keep the best, and return to it.

The sweep can be done in two ways. A stepped sweep moves the set-point in discrete steps and waits for settling at each one; its cost is the number of steps multiplied by the settling time. A fast sweep lets the array's own input capacitor charge or discharge through the converter while sampling V and I continuously, which captures the whole I-V curve in tens of milliseconds. The fast sweep is what most commercial inverters use for their periodic shade check, and it is well worth considering for a project rig, because it turns an expensive search into one cheap measurement.

Scanning
AdvantagesFinds the true global peak with certainty, because it looks everywhere. No tuning parameters. Deterministic and repeatable, which makes it easy to test and to certify.
DisadvantagesA stepped sweep is slow: a 0.5 V step over a 160 V string is 320 settling times. Power is lost during every sweep. If the shade moves during the sweep, the result is stale.
Where it fitsWhenever a fast capacitor-based sweep is possible. Also as the periodic safety net behind a cheaper method.

10.3 The window method: using what you already know

A full sweep wastes most of its effort on voltages where no peak can possibly be. Section 9.2 showed that peaks appear only near multiples of the module open-circuit voltage. That fact turns a blind sweep into a short list.

For a string of Nm modules, define Nm windows, the k-th spanning roughly 0.72·k·Voc,mod to 0.95·k·Voc,mod. Probe inside each window, compare the powers, and refine only in the winner. The number of expensive measurements falls from hundreds to about 2Nm.

The method works because the underlying structure is physical, not statistical. Each window corresponds to one more bypass group having switched out. That is why the rule survives changes of shading pattern, of irradiance, and of temperature.

Window scan
AdvantagesCost grows linearly with the number of modules, not with the voltage range. Deterministic: the same shade gives the same answer every time. No random numbers, no tuning constants, no convergence proof to argue about. Uses only the sensors already present.
DisadvantagesNeeds to know how many bypass groups are in series, and the module Voc. Both are known at commissioning, but both must be right. If two peaks are nearly equal in height, a single probe per window may pick the wrong one; probe twice, or refine the best two.
Where it fitsThe default recommendation for a string-level tracker, and the basis of the method in Chapter 11.

10.4 Metaheuristic search

The literature is dominated by population-based optimisers borrowed from computer science. The idea is always the same. Place several candidate operating points across the voltage range, measure the power at each, then move them according to a rule that mixes exploration with exploitation, and repeat until they converge.

Particle swarm optimisation is the most common. Each particle i holds a position Vi and a velocity, and is attracted both to its own best position and to the swarm's best:

(10.1) vi(k+1) = w·vi(k) + c1r1[ Pbest,i − Vi(k) ] + c2r2[ Gbest − Vi(k) ]
Vi(k+1) = Vi(k) + vi(k+1)

Others in the same family are grey wolf optimisation, cuckoo search with Lévy flights, artificial bee colony, firefly, teaching-learning-based optimisation, Jaya and vortex search. Comparative studies find that they mostly work, that the differences between them are smaller than the differences between tunings of any one of them, and that the initial placement of the candidates matters more than the update rule.

Metaheuristics
AdvantagesNeed no knowledge of the array at all: no module count, no Voc, no wiring information. Handle any curve shape, including ones the window rule does not describe. Easy to add to an existing tracker. Reported tracking efficiencies above 95 %, and above 99 % for well-tuned hybrids.
DisadvantagesEvery particle costs one settling time in every iteration, so the total cost is particles × iterations. Stochastic, so two runs on the same shade can give different answers and different times — awkward to test and to certify. Several tuning constants with no physical meaning. No guarantee of finding the global peak in finite time. Premature convergence onto a local peak is a documented failure of PSO and of several others.
Where it fitsArrays whose configuration is unknown or changes, research comparisons, and cases where the window assumption fails. Rarely the best engineering answer for a known, fixed string.

10.5 Hybrid, two-stage trackers

Almost every good published tracker is two-stage, whatever the paper calls it.

  1. A global stage that is coarse, wide and infrequent: a scan, a window probe, or a swarm. It answers the question which hill?
  2. A local stage that is fine, narrow and continuous: perturb and observe, incremental conductance, or fuzzy. It answers the question where exactly on this hill? and then holds position.

Splitting the problem this way is what makes the numbers work. The global stage runs for a few hundred milliseconds after a detection event. The local stage runs for the other 99.9 % of the time and is responsible for the steady-state efficiency. Reported combinations include neural network with incremental conductance at 97.5 % tracking efficiency, and swarm-based hybrids above 99 % under both standard and shaded conditions.

10.6 Reconfiguration: changing the curve instead of searching it

There is a completely different attack on the problem. Instead of finding the best point on a bad curve, change the wiring so that the curve is better.

Static reconfiguration physically relocates modules within a total-cross-tied array so that electrically adjacent modules are physically far apart. A shadow that falls on a compact physical area is then spread across many electrical rows. Because each row loses only a little, the staircase flattens into something close to a single peak. The relocation patterns are usually generated from a puzzle: Su Do Ku, magic square or futoshiki arrangements are common, because they guarantee that no two members of a row sit near each other. The wiring is never changed after installation, so there are no switches and nothing to fail.

Dynamic reconfiguration puts a matrix of switches between the modules and lets a controller re-wire the array in response to the measured shading pattern. It performs better than any static pattern, and it costs a switch matrix, its drivers, its control algorithm and its failure modes.

Reconfiguration
AdvantagesAttacks the mismatch loss itself, which no tracking algorithm can recover. Static versions need no electronics at all. Works together with any tracker.
DisadvantagesStatic patterns need long cable runs across the array, adding cost and resistive loss. They are optimised for assumed shade patterns, and behave less well for others. Dynamic versions add many switches in the main power path.
Where it fitsNew installations with known, repeating shade, such as row-to-row self shading. An excellent second experiment for a final year project, because the comparison against a fixed array is easy to measure.

11  The search question, answered

You asked: given a curve with several local maxima and one global maximum, what is the fastest and most computationally efficient technique to reach the global maximum? The short answer is that for this particular curve you should not use a general-purpose global optimiser at all. You should use the structure you already know.

11.1 State the problem properly first

Write it as an optimisation problem, and list what is actually known about it. Almost all of the answer falls out of this list.

11.2 The cost that actually matters

This is where most published comparisons go wrong. They count arithmetic operations or lines of code. Neither is the bottleneck. A modern microcontroller performs a particle swarm update in microseconds. What costs time is that every candidate voltage must be physically applied to the array and allowed to settle before its power can be measured. The unit of cost is one settling time, and everything else rounds to zero.

At a settling time of 20 ms, the same figures become: exhaustive sweep 6.4 s, particle swarm 1.8 s, DIRECT 0.8 s, window scan with refinement about 0.3 s. On a moving cloud edge, the difference between 1.8 s and 0.3 s is the difference between tracking the shade and chasing it.

Restating the objective

Do not ask for the algorithm with the lowest computational complexity. Ask for the algorithm that reaches the global peak in the fewest applied operating points. Those are different questions, and only the second one describes the hardware.

11.3 Why the popular answers are the wrong tools

Particle swarm, grey wolf, cuckoo search and the rest. These are designed for black-box functions in many dimensions where nothing is known about the landscape. Here the dimension is one and a great deal is known. They pay for generality you do not need: every particle costs a settling time in every iteration, they are stochastic so they give a different answer and a different duration each run, they carry tuning constants with no physical meaning, and they offer no guarantee of reaching the global peak in finite time. Premature convergence onto a local peak is a documented failure of several of them.

Gradient methods, Newton, and anything that follows a slope. These converge to the nearest stationary point, which is exactly the trap. They belong in the refinement stage, never in the global stage.

Simulated annealing. Correct in the limit, and far too slow: its guarantee needs a cooling schedule measured in thousands of evaluations.

Exhaustive search. Correct, deterministic and honest, but it spends most of its evaluations in voltage ranges where a peak cannot exist.

11.4 The method to use

Two answers, depending on what the hardware can do. Take the first if you can.

Answer A — if you can build a fast I-V sweep

Disconnect the load path for a few tens of milliseconds and let the array charge or discharge its own input capacitor from short circuit to open circuit, while sampling V and I at a few tens of kilohertz. You now hold several hundred points of the whole curve, bought with a single transient rather than several hundred settling times. Multiply, take the maximum, and go there. Then refine locally.

This is the true optimum for the problem as posed, because it collapses the search cost to one event. Its requirements are a fast ADC, a capacitor sized so that the sweep is slow enough to sample but fast enough not to lose energy, and care that the sweep does not disturb the load. Most commercial string inverters use exactly this for their periodic shade check.

Answer B — with ordinary hardware: bracket by structure, then search the bracket

Use the physical structure to reduce the candidate set to Nm, then use the provably optimal unimodal line search inside the winning bracket. The procedure, in words:

  1. Trigger. Enter the search only when the detector fires: a power deviation above the threshold, a bypass voltage step, or the periodic timer.
  2. Establish the scale. Take, or recall, the array open-circuit voltage. Divide by the number of series bypass groups Nm to get the effective Voc,mod. This is the only array knowledge the method needs.
  3. Probe the windows. For k = 1 to Nm, apply two set-points inside the k-th window, at about 0.78·k·Voc,mod and 0.90·k·Voc,mod, and record the power at each. Cost: 2Nm settling times. Move downward from high voltage to low, so that the array current rises gradually rather than in one jump.
  4. Select. Take the window with the highest measured power. If the best two are within the measurement noise of one another, keep both.
  5. Refine. Run a Fibonacci or golden-section search inside the winning window. Each evaluation shrinks the bracket by a factor of 0.618, so six to seven evaluations reduce a 17 V window to under 1 V, which is within 1 % of the peak power.
  6. Hand over. Give the result to incremental conductance with a small step, record the converged power as the new Pref, and return to normal tracking.

For a four-module string this is 8 + 7 = 15 applied operating points, about 0.3 seconds at a 20 ms settling time, with no random numbers and no tuning constants.

Why Fibonacci and not something cleverer

Inside one bypass segment the power curve has a single maximum. For that class of function, Fibonacci search is provably optimal: no method that only compares function values can guarantee a smaller interval of uncertainty for the same number of evaluations. Golden-section search gives up a negligible amount and does not need the number of evaluations to be fixed in advance, which makes it easier to code. Either is the right choice; there is nothing to be gained by looking further.

11.5 Complexity, and what is guaranteed

(11.2) global stage:   Θ( Nm ) evaluations
refinement:    Θ( log(L0 / ε) ) evaluations, bracket × 0.618 each time
total:          2Nm + 7, independent of the voltage range

The method finds the true global peak whenever two conditions hold: every peak lies inside its window, and the probe inside the tallest window returns a power larger than the probes in the others. The first condition is the published 70 % to 95 % rule and is reliable. The second can fail when two peaks are nearly equal, which is exactly the case where the penalty for choosing wrongly is smallest. Refining the best two windows removes the failure at a cost of seven more evaluations.

It is worth knowing what a fully general answer would look like, because your examiners may ask. For a black-box multimodal function on a bounded interval, no algorithm can guarantee the global maximum from finitely many samples without an assumption that limits how fast the function can change. With a Lipschitz constant L known, the Shubert-Piyavskii algorithm is optimal and needs O(L(b−a)/ε) evaluations. If L is unknown, the DIRECT algorithm subdivides the interval and searches all rates simultaneously; it converges to the global optimum and typically needs 30 to 60 evaluations here. DIRECT is therefore the correct answer if you must not assume anything about the array. It is still five times more expensive than using what you know.

The one-paragraph answer

The fastest technique is the one that spends the fewest settling times, because settling time, not arithmetic, is the cost. Do not search blindly: the number of local maxima is bounded by the number of series bypass groups, and each one sits near a known multiple of the module open-circuit voltage. Probe those Nm windows, pick the best, and finish with a Fibonacci or golden-section line search inside it, which is optimal for a single-peaked function. That is 2Nm + 7 operating points, deterministic and repeatable, against roughly 90 for particle swarm and 320 for an exhaustive sweep. If your hardware can capture the whole I-V curve in one capacitor transient, do that instead: it reduces the entire search to a single event.

C  Glossary

Bypass diode
A diode across a group of cells that carries the string current when the group cannot, limiting the group's reverse voltage to one diode drop.
Blocking diode
A diode in series with a string that prevents reverse current from other strings or from a battery.
GMPP / LMPP
Global and local maximum power point.
Hot spot
Localised heating in a reverse-biased cell that is dissipating the power generated by the healthy cells around it.
Mismatch loss
The power lost because series-connected devices are forced to share one current, or parallel devices one voltage.
MLPE
Module-level power electronics: DC optimisers and microinverters.
NOCT
Nominal operating cell temperature: 800 W/m², 20 °C ambient, 1 m/s wind. A more realistic reference than STC.
Partial shading
A condition in which the modules of one array receive different irradiance at the same time.
Settling time
The time the power stage needs after a set-point change before a measurement is meaningful. The unit of cost for every search method.
STC
Standard test conditions: 1000 W/m², 25 °C cell temperature, AM1.5 spectrum.
Staircase curve
The stepped I-V characteristic of a partially shaded string, caused by bypass diodes switching in one after another.
Tracking efficiency
Energy actually captured divided by energy available at the maximum power point, over a defined test.
