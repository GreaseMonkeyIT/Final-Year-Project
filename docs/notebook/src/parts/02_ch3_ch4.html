3  How light and heat move the curve

Two variables in the field change the curve minute by minute: the irradiance falling on the plane of the module, and the cell temperature. They act on different parts of the curve. This is the section that answers the question what sags, and what stays put.

3.1 Irradiance: the current scales, the knee barely moves

Irradiance G sets the photocurrent directly. The relation is close to linear:

(3.1) Iph(G, T) = (G / 1000) · [ Iph,STC + αI · (T − 25 °C) ]

Because Isc ≈ Iph, the short-circuit current follows the light almost exactly. Cut the irradiance to one fifth, and the short-circuit current falls to one fifth. The model confirms this to within rounding.

3.2 Why Voc moves so little

The reason is worth deriving, because the whole fractional-voltage family of trackers rests on it. Set I = 0 in the ideal equation and solve for V:

(3.2) Voc = a · ln( Iph / I0 + 1 ) ≈ a · ln( Iph / I0 )

Now change the irradiance from G1 to G2. Only Iph changes, and it changes in proportion, so:

(3.3) Voc(G2) − Voc(G1) = a · ln( G2 / G1 )

The voltage difference is logarithmic in the irradiance ratio, while the current difference is linear. For the reference module, a = 1.77 V. Halving the light therefore costs 1.77 × ln 2 = 1.23 V, which is 3.3 % of Voc. Halving the light costs 50 % of the current. That is the entire asymmetry in one line.

Answer to the question “what is constant in dim and strong light”

Nothing is exactly constant, but three quantities are nearly so:

  • the knee voltage, and therefore Voc and Vmp, which move only logarithmically;
  • the ratio Vmp/Voc, which stays between 0.826 and 0.845 over a ten-to-one light range on the reference module;
  • the ratio Imp/Isc, which stays between 0.928 and 0.930 over the same range.

The strongly varying quantities are Isc, Imp and Pmax, all of which are proportional to irradiance.

3.3 Temperature: the voltage collapses, the current creeps up

Temperature works the other way round. The band gap narrows slightly as silicon heats, so marginally more photons are absorbed and Isc rises a little. At the same time the reverse saturation current I0 rises steeply, because it depends on exp(−Eg/kT). A larger I0 means the diode swallows the photocurrent at a lower voltage, so Voc falls.

One sentence to remember

Light controls the current axis. Temperature controls the voltage axis. A tracker that assumes otherwise will chase the wrong variable.

4  Why maximum power point tracking exists

4.1 The load picks the operating point, not the panel

A panel does not choose where on its curve it sits. The load does. Connect a resistor R across the terminals, and the operating point is the intersection of the panel curve with the straight line I = V/R. Connect a battery, and the panel is clamped to the battery voltage, so the operating point is wherever the curve crosses that vertical line.

The figure makes the loss concrete. The reference module can deliver 265 W at STC. Wire it directly to a nominal 24 V lead-acid battery, which sits near 24 V while charging, and the panel is forced to 24 V. It then delivers 9.08 A, which is 217.8 W. You have thrown away 47 W, or 17.8 %, with no fault anywhere in the system.

4.2 The real statement: impedance matching

At the maximum power point the panel behaves, instantaneously, like a source feeding an optimum resistance:

(4.1) Ropt = Vmp / Imp = 30.97 / 8.559 = 3.62 Ω  (at STC)

Maximum power point tracking is therefore an impedance-matching problem. The tracker's job is to make the source see 3.62 Ω no matter what is really connected. And Ropt itself moves: at 200 W/m² it becomes 29.27 / 1.712 = 17.1 Ω, roughly five times larger. So the matching must be continuous, not a one-time adjustment.

4.3 The switching converter as an adjustable impedance

You cannot change the battery, and you cannot change the panel. You put a DC-DC converter between them. Seen from the panel, a lossless converter feeding a fixed load looks like a resistor whose value you set with the duty ratio D.

For a lossless converter driving a load of resistance R, the input resistance seen by the panel is:

(4.2) buck:     Rin = R / D²
boost:    Rin = R · (1 − D)²
buck-boost:  Rin = R · [ (1 − D) / D ]²

The buck can only make the source see a resistance smaller than the load. The boost can only make it look larger. The buck-boost can do both, which is why it, or a SEPIC, or a Cuk, is chosen when the panel voltage may sit on either side of the bus voltage. For a battery load, the same algebra applies to the equivalent operating resistance Vbat/Ibat.

4.4 The tracking loop and its timing

Every tracker, however clever, is the same three steps repeated forever.

  1. Sense. Measure the array voltage and the array current. Multiply to get power. Two sensors is the usual minimum. Some methods need only one.
  2. Decide. Compare the new measurement with the previous one, or with a model, and choose a new operating point.
  3. Act. Move the duty ratio, or move the reference voltage that an inner loop then regulates.

The timing of step 3 is the part most often got wrong in a first design. After you change the duty ratio, the input capacitor, the inductor and the array capacitance need time to settle. If you sample before the transient has decayed, you measure the transient and not the new operating point, and the algorithm makes a decision on noise. The perturbation period must be several times the settling time of the power stage. For a small converter this is typically 1 ms to 50 ms. This single number, the settling time, is what makes the search question in Chapter 11 interesting: it, and not processor speed, is the real cost of one trial point.

Control variable: duty ratio or reference voltage?

Perturbing D directly is simplest and needs no inner loop. Perturbing a reference voltage Vref, with a PI loop that forces Vpv to follow it, is slower to design but far better behaved: the step size in volts then means the same thing at every operating point, and the loop rejects load disturbances. Most production trackers use the reference-voltage form.

4.5 How a tracker is scored

Two numbers, and they are not the same.

(4.3) ηMPPT = ∫ Pactual(t) dt ⁄ ∫ Pmax,available(t) dt

Static tracking efficiency is measured with the light held constant. It is limited by the steady-state oscillation around the peak. A well-tuned hill climber reaches 99 % or better.

Dynamic tracking efficiency is measured while the light changes on a defined ramp, as in the EN 50530 test profiles. It is limited by how fast the tracker can follow. This is the number that separates good trackers from poor ones, and it is where a naive perturb-and-observe loses badly.

For a shading study you need a third number: the global tracking success rate, which is the fraction of shading patterns in which the tracker settles on the true global peak rather than a local one. A tracker can score 99 % static efficiency and still be sitting on the wrong peak, losing a third of the available power.
