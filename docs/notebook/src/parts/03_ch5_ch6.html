5  Tracking methods I: the offline family

5.1 A map of the families

There are more than a hundred published tracking methods. Almost all of them belong to one of three families, and the family tells you the strengths and the weaknesses before you read a single equation.

The offline family shares one idea: the maximum power point is not measured, it is estimated from something cheaper. That estimate is then used as a set-point. The panel is never asked whether the estimate was right.

5.2 Fractional open-circuit voltage

This method rests on the observation from Section 3.2: the ratio Vmp/Voc is nearly constant.

(5.1) Vmp ≈ kv · Voc    with kv ≈ 0.71 to 0.86 for silicon

For the reference module the model gives kv between 0.798 and 0.861 across the full range of 100 to 1000 W/m² and 0 to 65 °C. Choosing a fixed kv = 0.82 costs at most 1.85 % of the available power anywhere on that grid, and less than 0.7 % over most of it. That is a remarkable result for a method that needs one sensor and two lines of arithmetic.

How it runs. Every few seconds the converter is stopped, or a pilot cell is read, so that Voc can be measured. The set-point kv·Voc is then held until the next sample.

Fractional Voc
AdvantagesOne voltage sensor. No multiplication, no division, no memory of past states. Cannot oscillate. Cannot become unstable. Reaches the neighbourhood of the peak in one step, which makes it a good starting stage for a two-stage tracker.
DisadvantagesNever exactly at the peak. The array must be disconnected to measure Voc, which loses all power for a few milliseconds each time. kv drifts with ageing, temperature and technology. Under partial shade the relation breaks down completely, because Voc no longer describes the shape of a multi-peak curve.
Where it fitsVery small, very cheap systems: solar lanterns, garden lights, water pumps. Also the coarse first stage of a hybrid tracker. Also as a safe fallback if sensors fail.

5.3 Fractional short-circuit current

The mirror image. The ratio Imp/Isc is even steadier than the voltage ratio: the model holds it between 0.928 and 0.930 across the whole irradiance range.

(5.2) Imp ≈ ki · Isc    with ki ≈ 0.78 to 0.93

Using a fixed ki = 0.929 on the reference module costs at most 0.26 % of the available power. It is the more accurate of the two fractional methods.

Fractional Isc
AdvantagesMore stable coefficient than the voltage method. Insensitive to temperature.
DisadvantagesMeasuring Isc means shorting the array. That needs an extra switch rated for the full array current, which costs more than it saves. A current sensor is more expensive than a voltage divider. Same blindness to partial shade.
Where it fitsSystems that already have a short-circuit-capable switch, or that carry a small pilot cell whose Isc can be measured without disturbing the array.

5.4 Look-up tables and curve fitting

If you measure irradiance and cell temperature, you can look up Vmp in a table filled during commissioning, or evaluate a fitted polynomial in G and T. The response is immediate, because there is no search at all.

Look-up and curve fitting
AdvantagesFastest possible response to a change: one table read. No oscillation. Predictable behaviour, which suits certification.
DisadvantagesNeeds a pyranometer and a temperature sensor, both of which cost more than the tracker. The table is valid for one array, in one place, in one state of cleanliness and age. It cannot represent shading patterns at all, because the shading pattern is not a scalar.
Where it fitsLarge plants that already carry weather instruments, used as a coarse pre-position for a fine tracker. Space systems, where the environment is known exactly and reliability outranks accuracy.

6  Tracking methods II: hill climbing and beyond

The online family does not guess. It moves the operating point, watches what happens to the power, and uses the result to decide the next move. It is exact at steady state, and it needs no knowledge of the panel. It is also, without help, strictly local: it climbs the hill it is standing on.

6.1 Perturb and observe

The oldest and still the most used method. The rule is one sentence: change the operating voltage by a small step; if the power went up, take another step the same way; if the power went down, step the other way.

The step size is the whole design. A large step reaches the peak quickly but then oscillates across it with a large amplitude, wasting power in steady state. A small step settles tightly but takes many cycles to cross the curve, and responds slowly to a passing cloud. The reference module gives a feel for the cost: sitting 1 V away from Vmp costs 1.0 % of peak power, 2 V costs 5.0 %, and 3 V costs 13.4 %. The curve is flat at the top and steep on the right shoulder, so an overshoot to the right is much more expensive than the same overshoot to the left.

The drift problem. Perturb and observe cannot tell the difference between “the power rose because I moved” and “the power rose because a cloud cleared”. On a fast irradiance ramp it therefore reads a rising power, concludes its last move was correct, and keeps moving in a direction that is in fact wrong. The operating point drifts away from the peak. The standard cures are:

  • Variable step size. Make the step proportional to |dP/dV|, so it is large far from the peak and small near it. This is the single most effective improvement, and it is cheap.
  • Three-point weight comparison. Sample at three points before deciding, which rejects a monotone irradiance ramp.
  • Add a current measurement test. A change in I at constant V indicates irradiance change, not the effect of the perturbation.
Perturb and observe
AdvantagesNo model of the panel. No calibration. Works with any array. Trivial to code and to test. Reaches the true peak, not an estimate. Static tracking efficiency above 99 % with a good step size.
DisadvantagesAlways oscillates around the peak. Drifts on fast irradiance ramps. Needs both sensors. Stops at the first peak it finds, which under partial shade is very often the wrong one.
Where it fitsThe default for anything without shading. The local-refinement stage of nearly every global method, including the one recommended in Chapter 11.

6.2 Incremental conductance

This method uses the same hill, but computes the slope analytically instead of comparing two power readings. Differentiate P = V·I with respect to V:

(6.1) dP/dV = I + V · dI/dV

At the maximum power point the slope is zero, which gives the test that names the method:

(6.2) at the MPP:   dI/dV = − I / V
left of it:    dI/dV > − I / V   (increase V)
right of it:   dI/dV < − I / V   (decrease V)

In words: the incremental conductance dI/dV is compared with the negative of the instantaneous conductance I/V. In an implementation the derivatives become differences, ΔI/ΔV.

Incremental conductance
AdvantagesHas a true stop condition, so it can stop oscillating once the peak is reached. Distinguishes a change caused by its own perturbation from a change caused by irradiance, because it looks at ΔV and ΔI separately. Better dynamic tracking efficiency than plain P&O on ramps.
DisadvantagesNeeds a division, so more computation and more care with fixed-point arithmetic. The exact equality is never met with real ADC noise, so a tolerance band must be added — and that band re-introduces a small oscillation. Still strictly local.
Where it fitsThe standard choice for grid-tied string inverters and for anything where irradiance changes quickly. Also the usual refinement stage in global methods.

6.3 Hill climbing on the duty ratio

Identical logic to perturb and observe, but the perturbed variable is the duty ratio itself rather than a voltage reference. It removes the inner voltage loop, which saves a controller and some tuning.

The price is that the relation between duty ratio and array voltage is non-linear and depends on the load. A fixed step in D gives a small voltage step in one part of the range and a large one in another. The tracker is therefore well damped at one operating point and twitchy at another.

6.4 Ripple correlation control

A switching converter already makes the array voltage and current ripple at the switching frequency. Ripple correlation control uses that ripple as the perturbation, so no artificial perturbation is needed at all.

The insight is a sign test. Below the peak, power and voltage ripple in phase, so their time derivatives have the same sign. Above the peak they are in antiphase. The control law is therefore:

(6.3) dD/dt = − k · ( dP/dt ) · ( dV/dt )    k > 0

The product is positive below the peak and negative above it, so the duty ratio is driven until the product averages to zero, which happens only at the peak.

Ripple correlation control
AdvantagesConverges in a few switching periods, so it is one to three orders of magnitude faster than perturb and observe. No added perturbation, so no added steady-state loss. Proven to converge.
DisadvantagesNeeds analogue or fast-sampled measurement of the ripple, which means wideband sensing and careful layout. Sensitive to phase shift in the sensing chain: a filter that delays the ripple can invert the sign of the product and make the loop climb the wrong way. Still local.
Where it fitsModule-level converters and any design where the tracker must follow fast-moving shade edges. Attractive for a hardware-focused final year project, because the interesting work is analogue.

6.5 Extremum seeking control

A small sinusoidal dither at frequency ω is added to the set-point. The measured power is multiplied by the same sinusoid and low-pass filtered. The result is proportional to the local gradient, with the correct sign. An integrator then drives the gradient to zero. It is the formal, provable version of hill climbing, and it separates the estimation frequency from the control bandwidth, which is what gives it its good noise behaviour.

6.6 Fuzzy logic control

Instead of a fixed step size, the step is decided by a rule base. Two inputs are usual: the error E = ΔP/ΔV and its change ΔE. Each is mapped to linguistic labels such as negative big, zero, positive small. A rule table then selects a change in duty ratio, and the result is defuzzified to a number.

In practice a fuzzy controller is a smooth, non-linear, gain-scheduled hill climber. It takes large steps far from the peak and small steps near it, which is exactly the variable step size that plain perturb and observe wants, but expressed in a form that is easier to tune by hand than a formula.

Fuzzy logic
AdvantagesNo panel model. Handles the non-linearity gracefully. Small steady-state oscillation and fast approach at the same time. Tolerant of sensor noise.
DisadvantagesThe rule base and membership functions are chosen by the designer, so performance depends on skill and on trial. More memory and computation than P&O. Still local unless combined with a global search.
Where it fitsSystems with fast-changing conditions where the tuning effort is justified. Common as the refinement half of a hybrid global tracker.

6.7 Neural networks

A network is trained offline to map inputs such as irradiance, temperature, Voc and Isc onto Vmp. At run time it is a fixed function evaluation, so the response is immediate.

Neural network
AdvantagesOnce trained, it jumps straight to the answer with no search. Can absorb effects that no simple formula captures, including the array's own shading geometry if it was in the training set.
DisadvantagesNeeds a large, representative training set from the actual array. Must be retrained as the array ages or is modified. Behaves unpredictably outside the training distribution, which is precisely where an unusual shading pattern lives.
Where it fitsLarge installations that can afford the data collection, or as a pre-positioning stage that a hill climber then refines.

6.8 Sliding mode and model predictive control

Sliding mode control defines a switching surface, usually S = dP/dV = 0, and applies a discontinuous control law that forces the state onto that surface and keeps it there. It is robust against parameter error and load change, and it has a formal stability proof. Its weakness is chattering at the switching surface, which must be softened in practice.

Model predictive control uses a model of the converter to predict the array power for each candidate switching state over a short horizon, then applies the state that maximises a cost function. It gives an excellent dynamic response with no perturbation at all, but it needs an accurate converter model and a processor fast enough to evaluate the predictions inside one switching period.

6.10 How to choose

The decision is usually made by three questions, in this order.

  1. Can the array be shaded in normal use? If yes, no method in this chapter is sufficient on its own. You need a global stage from Chapter 10 with one of these as the refinement stage.
  2. How fast does the irradiance change? A fixed installation in a clear climate can use plain perturb and observe. Moving shade edges, or a vehicle-mounted array, need incremental conductance, ripple correlation, or a variable step.
  3. What is the cost target? Below a few watts, the fractional methods win because they need one sensor. Above that, the sensors are already paid for, so the arithmetic is free.
