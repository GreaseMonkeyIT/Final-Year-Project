11  The search question, answered

You asked: given a curve with several local maxima and one global maximum, what is the fastest and most computationally efficient technique to reach the global maximum? The short answer is that for this particular curve you should not use a general-purpose global optimiser at all. You should use the structure you already know.

11.1 State the problem properly first

Write it as an optimisation problem, and list what is actually known about it. Almost all of the answer falls out of this list.

11.2 The cost that actually matters

This is where most published comparisons go wrong. They count arithmetic operations or lines of code. Neither is the bottleneck. A modern microcontroller performs a particle swarm update in microseconds. What costs time is that every candidate voltage must be physically applied to the array and allowed to settle before its power can be measured. The unit of cost is one settling time, and everything else rounds to zero.

At a settling time of 20 ms, the same figures become: exhaustive sweep 6.4 s, particle swarm 1.8 s, DIRECT 0.8 s, window scan with refinement about 0.3 s. On a moving cloud edge, the difference between 1.8 s and 0.3 s is the difference between tracking the shade and chasing it.

Restating the objective

Do not ask for the algorithm with the lowest computational complexity. Ask for the algorithm that reaches the global peak in the fewest applied operating points. Those are different questions, and only the second one describes the hardware.

11.3 Why the popular answers are the wrong tools

Particle swarm, grey wolf, cuckoo search and the rest. These are designed for black-box functions in many dimensions where nothing is known about the landscape. Here the dimension is one and a great deal is known. They pay for generality you do not need: every particle costs a settling time in every iteration, they are stochastic so they give a different answer and a different duration each run, they carry tuning constants with no physical meaning, and they offer no guarantee of reaching the global peak in finite time. Premature convergence onto a local peak is a documented failure of several of them.

Gradient methods, Newton, and anything that follows a slope. These converge to the nearest stationary point, which is exactly the trap. They belong in the refinement stage, never in the global stage.

Simulated annealing. Correct in the limit, and far too slow: its guarantee needs a cooling schedule measured in thousands of evaluations.

Exhaustive search. Correct, deterministic and honest, but it spends most of its evaluations in voltage ranges where a peak cannot exist.

11.4 The method to use

Two answers, depending on what the hardware can do. Take the first if you can.

Answer A — if you can build a fast I-V sweep

Disconnect the load path for a few tens of milliseconds and let the array charge or discharge its own input capacitor from short circuit to open circuit, while sampling V and I at a few tens of kilohertz. You now hold several hundred points of the whole curve, bought with a single transient rather than several hundred settling times. Multiply, take the maximum, and go there. Then refine locally.

This is the true optimum for the problem as posed, because it collapses the search cost to one event. Its requirements are a fast ADC, a capacitor sized so that the sweep is slow enough to sample but fast enough not to lose energy, and care that the sweep does not disturb the load. Most commercial string inverters use exactly this for their periodic shade check.

Answer B — with ordinary hardware: bracket by structure, then search the bracket

Use the physical structure to reduce the candidate set to Nm, then use the provably optimal unimodal line search inside the winning bracket. The procedure, in words:

  1. Trigger. Enter the search only when the detector fires: a power deviation above the threshold, a bypass voltage step, or the periodic timer.
  2. Establish the scale. Take, or recall, the array open-circuit voltage. Divide by the number of series bypass groups Nm to get the effective Voc,mod. This is the only array knowledge the method needs.
  3. Probe the windows. For k = 1 to Nm, apply two set-points inside the k-th window, at about 0.78·k·Voc,mod and 0.90·k·Voc,mod, and record the power at each. Cost: 2Nm settling times. Move downward from high voltage to low, so that the array current rises gradually rather than in one jump.
  4. Select. Take the window with the highest measured power. If the best two are within the measurement noise of one another, keep both.
  5. Refine. Run a Fibonacci or golden-section search inside the winning window. Each evaluation shrinks the bracket by a factor of 0.618, so six to seven evaluations reduce a 17 V window to under 1 V, which is within 1 % of the peak power.
  6. Hand over. Give the result to incremental conductance with a small step, record the converged power as the new Pref, and return to normal tracking.

For a four-module string this is 8 + 7 = 15 applied operating points, about 0.3 seconds at a 20 ms settling time, with no random numbers and no tuning constants.

Why Fibonacci and not something cleverer

Inside one bypass segment the power curve has a single maximum. For that class of function, Fibonacci search is provably optimal: no method that only compares function values can guarantee a smaller interval of uncertainty for the same number of evaluations. Golden-section search gives up a negligible amount and does not need the number of evaluations to be fixed in advance, which makes it easier to code. Either is the right choice; there is nothing to be gained by looking further.

11.5 Complexity, and what is guaranteed

(11.2) global stage:   Θ( Nm ) evaluations
refinement:    Θ( log(L0 / ε) ) evaluations, bracket × 0.618 each time
total:          2Nm + 7, independent of the voltage range

The method finds the true global peak whenever two conditions hold: every peak lies inside its window, and the probe inside the tallest window returns a power larger than the probes in the others. The first condition is the published 70 % to 95 % rule and is reliable. The second can fail when two peaks are nearly equal, which is exactly the case where the penalty for choosing wrongly is smallest. Refining the best two windows removes the failure at a cost of seven more evaluations.

It is worth knowing what a fully general answer would look like, because your examiners may ask. For a black-box multimodal function on a bounded interval, no algorithm can guarantee the global maximum from finitely many samples without an assumption that limits how fast the function can change. With a Lipschitz constant L known, the Shubert-Piyavskii algorithm is optimal and needs O(L(b−a)/ε) evaluations. If L is unknown, the DIRECT algorithm subdivides the interval and searches all rates simultaneously; it converges to the global optimum and typically needs 30 to 60 evaluations here. DIRECT is therefore the correct answer if you must not assume anything about the array. It is still five times more expensive than using what you know.

The one-paragraph answer

The fastest technique is the one that spends the fewest settling times, because settling time, not arithmetic, is the cost. Do not search blindly: the number of local maxima is bounded by the number of series bypass groups, and each one sits near a known multiple of the module open-circuit voltage. Probe those Nm windows, pick the best, and finish with a Fibonacci or golden-section line search inside it, which is optimal for a single-peaked function. That is 2Nm + 7 operating points, deterministic and repeatable, against roughly 90 for particle swarm and 320 for an exhaustive sweep. If your hardware can capture the whole I-V curve in one capacitor transient, do that instead: it reduces the entire search to a single event.

C  Glossary

Bypass diode
A diode across a group of cells that carries the string current when the group cannot, limiting the group's reverse voltage to one diode drop.
Blocking diode
A diode in series with a string that prevents reverse current from other strings or from a battery.
GMPP / LMPP
Global and local maximum power point.
Hot spot
Localised heating in a reverse-biased cell that is dissipating the power generated by the healthy cells around it.
Mismatch loss
The power lost because series-connected devices are forced to share one current, or parallel devices one voltage.
MLPE
Module-level power electronics: DC optimisers and microinverters.
NOCT
Nominal operating cell temperature: 800 W/m², 20 °C ambient, 1 m/s wind. A more realistic reference than STC.
Partial shading
A condition in which the modules of one array receive different irradiance at the same time.
Settling time
The time the power stage needs after a set-point change before a measurement is meaningful. The unit of cost for every search method.
STC
Standard test conditions: 1000 W/m², 25 °C cell temperature, AM1.5 spectrum.
Staircase curve
The stepped I-V characteristic of a partially shaded string, caused by bypass diodes switching in one after another.
Tracking efficiency
Energy actually captured divided by energy available at the maximum power point, over a defined test.
