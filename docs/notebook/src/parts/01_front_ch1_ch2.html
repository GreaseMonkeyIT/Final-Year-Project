Implementation of MPPT for PV Arrays under Partial Shading Conditions

A working notebook · built from first principles · all curves computed from the single-diode model

This notebook starts at the physics of one silicon cell and ends at the algorithm you will implement. Nothing is assumed except school-level circuit theory. Every I-V and P-V curve in these pages is not a sketch: each one is solved numerically from the five-parameter single-diode equation for a real 265 W module, so the numbers you read off the axes are the numbers the model gives.

How the book is arranged

The order follows the physical build-up of the problem. Chapters 1 to 3 describe one cell and what moves its curve. Chapter 4 shows why a tracker is needed at all. 1  The photovoltaic cell

1.1 What light does inside silicon

A solar cell is a large-area diode made from doped silicon. Its top layer is n-type: it has extra free electrons. Its lower, thicker layer is p-type: it has extra holes. Where the two layers meet, electrons diffuse into the p side and holes diffuse into the n side. They leave behind fixed charged atoms. This creates a thin region with no free carriers and a strong built-in electric field. This region is the depletion region, and its field is the working part of the cell.

Three things must happen for the cell to deliver current.

  1. Absorption. A photon enters the silicon and gives its energy to an electron. The photon must carry at least the band-gap energy, which is 1.12 eV for silicon. This corresponds to a wavelength of about 1100 nm. Photons with less energy pass through and do nothing. Photons with much more energy create one electron-hole pair and waste the surplus as heat.
  2. Separation. The freed electron and the hole it left behind must be pulled apart before they recombine. The built-in field of the depletion region does this. It pushes electrons toward the n side and holes toward the p side.
  3. Collection. The separated carriers must reach the metal contacts. The front contact is a grid of thin fingers, so it collects current without blocking much light. The rear contact usually covers the whole back surface.

1.2 Why a cell behaves as a current source with a diode across it

Two currents flow inside an illuminated cell at the same time, and they flow in opposite directions.

The first is the photocurrent Iph. It is set by how many usable photons arrive each second. Double the light, and you double the number of electron-hole pairs, so you double the photocurrent. The photocurrent is therefore almost exactly proportional to irradiance. It hardly depends on the cell's terminal voltage at all, because the built-in field sweeps the carriers out regardless.

The second is the ordinary diode current Id. The cell is still a p-n junction. When the terminals sit at a positive voltage, that voltage forward-biases the junction and pushes a recombination current back across it. This current grows exponentially with voltage.

The terminal current is the difference:

(1.1) I = Iph − Id(V)

That single line explains the whole shape of the I-V curve, and it is worth pausing on. At low terminal voltage the diode is barely conducting, so Id is negligible and I ≈ Iph. The cell looks like a current source. As the terminal voltage rises, Id grows exponentially and eats into the output. At one particular voltage the diode swallows the entire photocurrent, the terminal current reaches zero, and no more voltage is available. That voltage is the open-circuit voltage.

The one idea to carry forward

A solar cell is a light-controlled current source with a diode permanently connected across it. Light sets the height of the current source. The diode sets the voltage at which that current disappears. Everything else in this book follows from those two sentences.

2  The equivalent circuit and the I-V curve

2.1 The ideal cell

The simplest useful circuit has two elements: the photocurrent source and the junction diode.

Applying the Shockley diode equation to (1.1) gives the ideal characteristic:

(2.1) I = Iph − I0 · [ exp( V / (n·Vt) ) − 1 ]
Vt = k·T / q ≈ 25.7 mV at 25 °C

Here I0 is the reverse saturation current, which measures how leaky the junction is; n is the ideality factor, normally between 1 and 2; k is the Boltzmann constant; T is the absolute cell temperature; and q is the electron charge. Vt is the thermal voltage. For a module of Ns cells in series, replace n·Vt with n·Ns·Vt.

2.2 The five-parameter model

Two parasitic elements must be added before the model matches a real device.

Series resistance Rs collects every ohmic loss along the current path: the bulk silicon, the emitter sheet, the contact between metal and silicon, the fingers, the busbars and the ribbons. It is small, usually a few hundred milliohms for a module, but the current through it is large.

Shunt resistance Rsh represents every leakage path that lets current bypass the junction: edge leakage around the cell perimeter, cracks, and defects that short the junction locally. It should be large. A poor cell has a low shunt resistance.

Writing Kirchhoff's current law at the internal node gives the equation that the rest of the book uses:

(2.2) I = Iph − I0 · [ exp( (V + I·Rs) / a ) − 1 ] − (V + I·Rs) / Rsh
a = n · Ns · k · T / q   (the modified ideality factor)

Equation 2.2 is implicit: I appears on both sides and inside an exponential. It has no solution in elementary functions. In practice you solve it point by point with a root-finder, or in closed form with the Lambert W function. Every curve in this notebook was solved with a bracketing root-finder to a tolerance of 10−12 A.

2.3 The four landmarks of the curve

Four points describe almost everything you need.

Short-circuit current, Isc
The current when the terminals are shorted, so V = 0. The diode is unbiased and takes almost nothing, so Isc ≈ Iph. This is the largest current the cell can deliver. It is proportional to irradiance.
Open-circuit voltage, Voc
The voltage when no current is drawn, so I = 0. The diode is now consuming the whole photocurrent. Setting I = 0 in 2.1 and solving gives Voc = a·ln(Iph/I0 + 1). This is the largest voltage the cell can produce.
Maximum power point (MPP), at Vmp and Imp
The single point on the curve where the product V·I is largest. At both ends of the curve the power is zero, because one factor is zero. Somewhere between them there is a maximum. Finding and holding this point is the entire subject of this project.
Fill factor, FF
How square the curve is: FF = (Vmp·Imp) / (Voc·Isc). A perfect rectangle would give 1. Good silicon modules reach 0.75 to 0.82. Laboratory cells reach about 0.85. A falling fill factor is the standard symptom of a resistance problem.

Why the MPP must exist

Power is zero at V = 0 because there is no voltage. Power is zero at V = Voc because there is no current. Power is positive in between, and the curve is smooth. A continuous function that is zero at both ends and positive in the middle must have a maximum inside. Under uniform light there is exactly one such maximum. Under partial shade there can be several, and that is the whole difficulty of this project.

2.4 The two-diode model

The single-diode model is slightly wrong at low light. The reason is that recombination in the depletion region follows a different law from recombination in the bulk. The two-diode model gives each mechanism its own diode: one with n1 ≈ 1 for the bulk, and one with n2 ≈ 2 for the depletion region.

2.5 What the parasitic resistances do to the curve

The two resistances damage two different parts of the curve, and this is how you tell them apart on a flash-test report.

Series resistance pulls the curve down near the open-circuit end. It costs you a voltage of I·Rs at every operating point, and the current is highest at the knee, so the knee suffers most. Voc is untouched, because at open circuit no current flows through Rs. Isc is barely touched. The fill factor falls.

Shunt resistance tilts the flat part of the curve near short circuit. A low Rsh steals current in proportion to voltage, so the curve slopes downward instead of running flat. Isc is barely touched, but Voc falls and the fill factor falls.

Diagnostic rule

Look at the slope of the measured curve at the two ends. The slope near Voc is roughly −1/Rs. The slope near Isc is roughly −1/Rsh. Those two slopes are the fastest field estimate of both parasitic resistances, and they need no curve fitting.
