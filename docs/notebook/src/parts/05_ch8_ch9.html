8  What partial shade does to the circuit

8.1 Where shade comes from

Partial shading means that the modules of one array receive different irradiance at the same moment. The common causes divide into two groups by time scale.

Passing cloud edgesoft, moving, largeseconds to minutesno

A hard shadow is the dangerous kind. A cloud reduces irradiance to perhaps 20 % over a wide area, so the modules stay similar to one another. A pole casts a sharp shadow that puts one cell at 5 % while its neighbour stays at 100 %. That difference, inside one series group, is what does the damage.

8.2 The series bottleneck

Recall the series rule: the current is common. A cell that receives one fifth of the light can generate only one fifth of the photocurrent. If the string tries to carry more current than that, the shaded cell simply cannot supply it from light. The extra current must still pass through the cell, because there is nowhere else for it to go.

To pass a current larger than its photocurrent, the cell must go to a negative terminal voltage. It stops being a source and becomes a load. This is the single most important sentence in this chapter.

8.3 Reverse bias and the hot spot

Follow what happens as the string current is pushed above the shaded cell's photocurrent. The cell voltage falls through zero and goes negative. At first the reverse current is limited by the shunt resistance. Then, at a reverse voltage of typically 10 to 30 V for a silicon cell, avalanche breakdown begins and the cell conducts freely in reverse.

The cell is now dissipating |V| × I as heat. Two ceilings limit that number:

  • the reverse voltage at which the cell breaks down, which is a property of the cell;
  • the voltage the healthy cells in the same bypass group can produce, because they are the source driving the current.

For a 20-cell group at the reference operating point, the healthy cells can generate about 20 × 0.516 V × 8.56 A ≈ 88 W. Nearly all of that can end up in one shaded cell, in an area of about 150 cm², under a glass sheet. Cell temperatures above 150 °C are reported in hot-spot tests. The consequences are delamination, cracked glass, browning of the encapsulant, melted solder joints and, in the worst case, fire.

8.4 Bypass diodes

The cure is to give the current another path. A bypass diode is connected across a group of cells, with the opposite polarity to the cells. In normal operation the group produces a positive voltage, so the diode is reverse biased and does nothing. When the group is forced negative by about 0.5 V, the diode conducts and carries the string current around the group. The group's reverse voltage is then clamped to one diode drop instead of tens of volts.

Two design numbers matter.

Group size. Protection improves as the group gets smaller, because a smaller group can generate less power to dump into a shaded cell. The classic guidance is a maximum of about 15 cells per diode for full protection. Commercial practice uses 20 to 24 cells per diode, which is a compromise between safety and the cost of diodes and junction-box terminals. Half-cut modules improve on this without more diodes, because each half carries half the current.

Diode dissipation. A conducting bypass diode carries the entire string current. At 8.56 A, a Schottky diode at 0.45 V dissipates 3.9 W; a PN diode at 0.9 V dissipates 7.7 W. That heat is generated inside a sealed plastic junction box on the back of a hot module. Bypass diodes are one of the most common field failures, and they fail in two ways:

  • Short circuit, the usual thermal-runaway outcome. The substring is permanently removed, so the module loses a third of its output and nobody notices for months.
  • Open circuit, often after a lightning surge. The protection is gone, and the next hard shadow produces a hot spot.

8.5 What actually gets overloaded

It is worth being precise here, because “the shaded module overloads the others” is a common but loose statement. The healthy modules are not driven beyond their ratings by a shadow. Three real overloads do occur.

  1. Thermal overload of the shaded cell. Described above. This is the dangerous one, and bypass diodes exist to prevent it.
  2. Thermal overload of the bypass diode. Several watts, continuously, in a sealed box, whenever the shadow is present. Long daily shading from a fixed obstruction is much harder on a diode than an occasional cloud.
  3. Reverse current into a weak parallel string. If several strings are paralleled and one string's voltage collapses under shade, the healthy strings drive current backwards into it. The weak string then dissipates instead of generating.

There is also a fourth effect which is not an overload but is often mistaken for one. When the tracker sits on a low-voltage peak, the string current is close to Isc while the string voltage is low. Cables, connectors and fuses therefore carry near-maximum current while the array delivers little power. Nothing is damaged, but the resistive losses are as high as at full output, and the useful output is not.

8.6 Parallel strings and blocking diodes

A blocking diode in series with each string prevents reverse current from entering a weak or shaded string. It solves item 3 above. Its cost is a permanent forward drop in every string, at all times, which is a continuous loss of roughly 0.5 V out of several hundred. Modern practice usually prefers string fuses, or simply an inverter with one tracker per string, because a fuse costs nothing when nothing is wrong. Blocking diodes remain common in small battery systems, where they also stop the battery discharging into the array at night.

8.7 Damage, standards and ageing

Hot-spot endurance is a type-approval requirement. In IEC 61215 the hot-spot test (MQT 09) deliberately shades cells and runs the module at short circuit for several hours to show that it survives. Passing that test does not mean shading is harmless; it means a single event will not destroy the module.

Repeated shading shortens life in slower ways as well: thermal cycling of solder joints, local encapsulant browning, and accelerated degradation of the affected cells. A module that is shaded for two hours every day for twenty years experiences about 15 000 hot-spot cycles.

Design consequence

A global tracking algorithm is not only an energy-yield feature. By keeping the array off operating points where a shaded substring is deeply reverse biased, and by choosing a peak on the correct side of a bypass transition, the tracker also influences how much heat is put into the weakest cell. Tracking and reliability are not separate problems.

9  The multi-peak characteristic

9.1 How the staircase forms

Now put the two rules together. Take three modules in series at 1000, 600 and 300 W/m², each with a bypass diode. Sweep the string current from zero upward and see what each module contributes.

  • Below 2.76 A (the short-circuit current of the 300 W/m² module), all three modules generate. All three voltages add. The string behaves normally, but at the current of the weakest module.
  • Between 2.76 and 5.52 A, the weakest module cannot supply the current. Its bypass diode conducts. It now contributes about −0.7 V instead of +30 V. The string voltage drops by roughly 31 V in one step.
  • Above 5.52 A, the middle module drops out in the same way, and only the fully lit module remains.

Each bypass transition is a step down in voltage at almost constant current. The I-V curve becomes a staircase.

Multiply point by point by the voltage and the staircase becomes a range of hills.

9.2 Counting and placing the peaks

Two rules, both useful when you write the search algorithm.

(9.1) number of local peaks ≤ number of distinct irradiance levels in the string
number of local peaks ≤ number of bypass-diode groups in series

Both bounds hold at once, so the smaller applies. A string of four modules with one bypass diode each has at most four peaks, whatever the shadow does. This is the fact that makes the search tractable: the number of candidates is small, known in advance, and fixed by the hardware.

The second rule concerns position. Each peak occurs near the point where one more group has just been bypassed, so the peaks appear at roughly regular voltage intervals of about one module's Vmp. Published surveys place them at multiples of 70 % to 85 % of the module open-circuit voltage, with the two rightmost peaks between 75 % and 95 %. The model agrees: for a four-module string at 1000, 800, 450 and 250 W/m², the peaks fall at 77 %, 83 %, 88 % and 89 % of k × Voc,module.

Read the last row carefully, because it is the most instructive. One heavily shaded module out of four costs the string 26 % of its power, and the best operating point is now at 92 V instead of 124 V. A tracker that stayed near the old voltage would find the smaller peak and lose much more.

9.3 Why the classical trackers fail

Perturb and observe and incremental conductance both use only local information: the sign of dP/dV at the present operating point. On a multi-peak curve that sign tells you which way the nearest hilltop is. It says nothing about whether a taller hill exists elsewhere. Once the tracker reaches any hilltop, dP/dV = 0, and every rule it has says stop.

The cost is not small. For the three-module string above:

A quarter of the available power, lost by an algorithm that is working exactly as designed. And which peak the tracker lands on depends only on where it happened to be when the shadow arrived, so the loss is unpredictable from day to day.

9.4 How much energy is really at stake

Three different losses are often confused. Separating them tells you which one your project can actually address.

LossFor the 1000 / 600 / 300 stringCan MPPT recover it?
Loss of light795 W unshaded → 499 W of module-level potentialNo. The photons are gone.
Mismatch loss from series connection499 W → 337 W at the string's own global peakOnly by changing the architecture: optimisers, microinverters, or reconfiguration.

Your algorithm owns the third row. On this example it is worth 84 W out of 795 W, which is 11 % of the unshaded rating and 25 % of what is actually available. Field studies of shaded residential arrays generally report annual gains between 3 % and 15 % from global tracking, depending on how often the array is shaded.
